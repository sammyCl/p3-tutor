# A question outside the learner's scope

Sometimes the learner asks for something that sits beyond what they know, or beyond what their mission needs: a derivation, a proof, the theory under a tool, an advanced technique.
Refusing leaves them stuck, and answering in full buries them in material they cannot use yet.
So give the question a little, say honestly why the rest waits, and point them at something close to it that they can do now.

## Is it out of scope?

Read `MISSION.md`, `NOTES.md` and the learning records first.
A question is out of scope when either is true:

- **Past their level:** the full answer rests on things the notes and records say they have not learned yet, such as a branch of math, a programming skill, or an earlier idea in the subject.
- **Past their mission:** the mission does not need it, or lists it as out of scope.

When a question is in scope, this skill does not apply; answer it normally.

## The reply

Write it in this order, each part short, in plain words, following `PREFERENCES.md`:

1. **The basic idea.** Two or three sentences on what the thing is and what it does, at their level, general idea first.
   Keep it true as far as it goes, and say in one clause what you are leaving out.
   Define any new term the first time you use it.
2. **Why the rest waits.** One or two sentences that name the specific things the full answer rests on that they have not learned yet, from their notes and records, not a vague "it's advanced".
   When it is true, add that their mission does not need it.
   Say it as a fact about where they are now, never as a judgment of them.
3. **A topic to try instead.** One related topic or task that is within their reach now and moves them toward their mission, and why it is the useful next step.
   Make it concrete: something to build, try, test or read, tied to their own project when you can.
4. **The way in, for later.** In one sentence, what they would learn first to go deeper, and offer it as a next lesson.
5. **A check.** End with one short question that has them use the basic idea or the topic you suggested.

Keep the whole reply under about 250 words.

## No math

The learner asked for explanations without complicated math, so the reply holds none of it, even when the question asks for it:

- no equations, formulas, derivations or proofs, not even one line or a "simplified" version;
- no symbols standing for quantities: no Greek letters, no variables such as x or θ, no subscripts, no fractions or ratios written as math, no sums, logs, gradients or probabilities written as expressions;
- no code beyond their level.

Describe in words what the math does and why it matters, the way you would to someone who has not met it.
For example, asked to derive why a sorting algorithm takes the time it does on average, say "on average each split cuts the list roughly in half, so the work grows a little faster than the list does", not a recurrence.

## Check before you send

Reread the reply and check that:

- it holds no equation, formula, symbol for a quantity, or derivation step;
- the "why it waits" names specific things they have not learned, from their notes;
- it suggests one related topic within their reach, and says why it helps;
- it is in plain words, under about 250 words, and ends with one question.

Rewrite any part that fails.
